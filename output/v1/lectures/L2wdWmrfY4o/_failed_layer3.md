## §5. Scientists vs. engineers; Josephson and Edison [15:23]

`§5.p1` The scientist explains that which exists. There are a lot of wannabe scientists, at MIT, most of them in the School of Engineering. I often say eighty percent of the faculty in the School of Engineering are wannabe scientists. There's only about twenty percent engineers on the faculty in the School of Engineering, in terms of people who can actually go out and solve a real problem. If you present a problem to a scientist, he will throw out all the hard part, so he can solve it in closed form with a fourth-order differential equation or whatever sophisticated technique. In fact, that's what you've learned in many of your classes to do.

`§5.p2` Whereas an engineer — Thomas Edison was an engineer. Edison was intuitive, he just kind of did things and made them work, and he created things that never were. He didn't understand why they worked, and he said it was ninety-nine percent perspiration, one percent inspiration. A scientist would much rather be on the ninety-nine percent inspiration and one percent perspiration — that's why they do calculations. It's not a lot of heavy lifting, but it takes deep intellect in a very narrow field.

`§5.p3` Herman Fishbach [Feshbach] was head of physics twenty years ago, and Phil Morris [Morse] was his mentor, a faculty member here. They wrote a book called *Theoretical Modern Physics* or something — it's basically a bunch of mathematical techniques to solve very complex equations. It's a very famous book. But most physicists will throw out the difficult terms so they can solve the equation explicitly, and then they thump their chests and say, *I solved this problem.* Well, they solved a mathematical problem, they didn't necessarily solve a real problem.

`§5.p4` Does anybody know the story of Brian Josephson? Does anybody know what the Josephson effect is? Brian Josephson was a graduate student about 1962 — I think at Cambridge, might have been Oxford, somewhere in England — and he went to see his thesis advisor. He was in physics, and he wanted to know what should he do his thesis on. His professor was busy, so he said, *go solve this problem*. It had to do with electrons. So Brian Josephson goes out and writes down the differential equation. Most people who had solved this problem in the past — it was sort of a standard problem in physics — knew that you throw out one term because it was inconsequential. Brian Josephson, being young, not knowing much about the world, kept the term in and went through the harder problem of solving it with that term, and he discovered the Josephson effect. The Josephson effect is basically tunneling of electrons through insulating layers. At age twenty-four or so he won the Nobel Prize.

`§5.p5` I met him when he was about thirty, because of my thesis advisor by 1965, with Margaret MacVicar — ever heard of Margaret MacVicar? The MacVicar Faculty Fellows at MIT. Margaret was a physics student who couldn't get into the physics department at MIT, because they won't admit their own. That's why Feynman went to Princeton — MIT wouldn't admit him into the physics department. Margaret came to the materials science department and wanted to do a doctoral thesis. She went to Bob Rose and said, *what should I do my thesis on?* He said, *go up and see Puffer*, who was the technician up in the lab. Puffer trained me in the lab. Puffer was a genius, should have gotten an MIT PhD, but he grew up in the Depression and his family couldn't afford to send him to school. Puffer showed her how to grow a single crystal of niobium, lay down an insulating layer on it, put some electrodes on it, soak it in liquid helium, and do tunneling experiments and show the Josephson effect. So Brian Josephson did the calculation saying *electrons can go through insulating layers*, and Bob Rose and Margaret did the experiment.

`§5.p6` Margaret graduated in two and a half years, went off as a Kennedy Fellow or something in England, came back here as an assistant professor of physics, and rose to Dean of Undergraduate Education, but died of brain cancer at about age forty-one or forty-two. In any case: a scientist explains that which exists, and Brian Josephson made the mistake of solving the harder problem and not throwing out the supposedly insignificant term, which turned out not to be insignificant. On the other hand, engineers create things that never were, and they don't always know why they work.

`§5.p7` How do you find tungsten as the light bulb filament — actually Edison didn't, that was his first useful filament for a light bulb, which was what? Carbonized horsehair. He just burned up horsehair and turned it into a carbon filament — had life like eleven hours if you were lucky.

## §6. Webster's, West Point, and the etymology of engineer [22:51]

`§6.p1` Now you can go to Webster's dictionary, which is always the sterile way to do it. *Engineer*, noun: the builder of military engines. That's the number one definition. Why? In the history of engineering — this is posed by Encyclopedia Britannica, so it might be from about 1985 — it turns out the first engineering school in the United States was West Point. Until 1823 or 1824, when they were building the Erie Canal in New York State, and Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute started the second engineering school in the country, and they had one curriculum: civil engineering, to distinguish it from the only type of engineering known before that, which was military engineering. So now you know where we get the term *civil engineering*.

`§6.p2` In any case, the word goes back to the 14th century in the French, which means a person who designs and invents or contrives. It actually comes from the French word meaning *contrive* — a designer, a builder of engines. These could be catapult engines or breastworks and dams. An engineer would build military facilities. The commandant of West Point until 1845 was required to come from the Army Corps of Engineers. They changed that, but that was part of the history.

`§6.p3` *Engineer*, verb, means to act as an engineer in laying out construction or management of something like some great dams. To guide the course of, manage or supervise during production and development. What's the interesting word in there that you don't usually associate with engineering? Management. And I'm going to tell you the history of Harvard's first engineering school. They tried to purchase MIT three times, 1873 to 1917, and were unsuccessful because the Supreme Court of Massachusetts turned it down. So they built, on the land that Andrew Carnegie had bought for them in Allston, their engineering school — and today it's known as the Harvard Business School. I'll tell you that story.

`§6.p4` **Student:** Is Allston in Cambridge?

`§6.p5` Allston is across the river — Allston, Massachusetts, not in Cambridge.

`§6.p6` *Engineering*: the science by which the properties of matter and the sources of energy in nature are made useful to man in structures, machines, and products. Your definitions, both of your definitions, had some of these types of things. So that's a common definition.

## §8. Maslow, military roads, and the Space Shuttle [30:36]

`§8.p1` Going back: the Romans, their roads are still working after 2,000 years. These different people came along and they improved on the Roman system by making it cheaper and faster to build, but it doesn't have the same longevity as the Roman roads. `§8.p2` Why did the Romans build these roads? It was for their military, to be able to get the supplies and the soldiers where they wanted. Anyone ever heard of Maslow's hierarchy of needs? What's Maslow's hierarchy of needs?

`§8.p3` *[Shows simplified Maslow's hierarchy.]* This is a simplified Maslow — there's a more complex one, this is just off Wikipedia. The Romans built roads as part of their military security program. Why did we build the interstate highway system in the 1950s — the Eisenhower interstate highway system? He liked the autobahn, but there was another military reason that the generals wanted it. We had missiles on trucks. The only way you could keep the Soviets from targeting our launch sites was to move the launch sites. The Soviets did the same thing — they built mobile launch sites, and we built mobile launch sites. We built a huge interstate highway system to be able to move the missiles around. It's harder in the Soviet Union to do that, it's a little larger.

`§8.p4` The first level of Maslow is physiological needs — food, shelter, clothing. The next one is safety and security — that's your military security. The Romans built roads for the second level on Maslow; we built the interstate highway system for the same reason. It was a military endeavor.
